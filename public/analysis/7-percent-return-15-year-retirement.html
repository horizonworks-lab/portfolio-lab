If You Retire in 15 Years, What Return Should You Assume?

If you plan to retire in 15 years, you need a return assumption for your savings between now and then. A 7% annual return is a common choice. But how often did investors actually earn that over a 15-year period, and how much should you rely on it?

One way to think about the question is whether 7% was achievable historically. Another way is to ask how often it was achieved. A third way is to ask what level of confidence you need before an assumption feels reasonable for your plan.

For some investors, an assumption may be reasonable if it occurred in roughly half of historical periods. Others may want it to hold in three quarters of periods before building a retirement plan around it.

Historical Outcomes

To explore this question, I analyzed every rolling 15-year period available in the historical dataset using a 100% S&P 500 portfolio. Each period represents an investor who stays invested for 15 years from a different starting year.

Rather than focusing on a single average return, the figures below show the full distribution of historical outcomes. All figures are inflation-adjusted (real) annualized returns.

  • Worst outcome: -0.5% real annualized return
  • 25th percentile: +3.2%
  • Median outcome: +7.4%
  • 75th percentile: +10.5%
  • Best outcome: +15.1%

Half of all historical 15-year periods produced real returns above 7.4% per year.

However, one quarter of historical periods produced less than 3.2% per year.

The more interesting observation may be that the answer depends on what "reasonable" means to you.

If you are comfortable with an assumption that was achieved in roughly half of historical periods, then 7% appears broadly consistent with the historical record.

If you prefer an assumption that was achieved in at least 75% of historical periods, a figure closer to 3.2% may be more appropriate.

Fifteen years is long enough for compounding to matter, but still short enough that starting year had a meaningful impact on outcomes. For someone with a fixed retirement date, that spread matters: the same savings could have grown very differently depending on when the 15 years started.

Two Things to Keep in Mind

First, these are real (inflation-adjusted) returns. If your plan uses 7% as a nominal figure, it corresponds to a lower real return after inflation, so the two should not be compared directly.

Second, an annualized return describes a single amount invested at the start and held for the full 15 years. Someone who saves regularly each month has money invested for different lengths of time, so their outcome can differ.

Key Takeaways

  • A 7% real return assumption was achieved in roughly half of historical 15-year periods.
  • With 15 years until retirement, outcomes varied substantially depending on starting year, from -0.5% to +15.1% per year.
  • Whether 7% is a reasonable assumption depends largely on how much uncertainty you are willing to accept in your plan.

Explore Your Own Scenarios

Historical data can help illuminate trade-offs, but it cannot determine which outcome an investor should prefer.

This analysis looked only at a 100% S&P 500 portfolio. Different conclusions may emerge when looking at portfolios that include bonds, treasury bills, gold or other asset allocations.

Some investors are willing to accept a lower probability of success in exchange for higher long-term upside. Others prefer to sacrifice some upside in order to improve outcomes in less favorable historical periods.
